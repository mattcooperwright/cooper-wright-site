There are two projects active at IDEO with the same problem. It's a pattern i've seen repeatedly over the last 15 years at IDEO.

The first project is with global FMCG company. They've hired a market research firm to understand the current state of customers on a weight-management journey. They came back with seven *Personas*, the spectrum runs from "not engaged at all" through to "designing my own nutrient breakfast". It's solid, and robust work. But it's emphasis is on segmenting the world into sizable chunks.

The second project is for a supplements startup. IDEO Colleague Rita Nicolau, who's leading the work, told me about the *Personas* the client had already bought before we started. We both saw the same shortcomings:behavioural descriptions in neat rows, "Meet the Health nerd who researches everything and trusts experts." Or, here we find the "Marathon runner who optimises performance." These are useful pointers, they don't create a spark for design.

Why not? Because they describe **what** people do but not *why* they do it. Worse still, they tend to freeze people in place at the moment of measurement.

## What's missing

When Rita read those personas she said something I keep coming back to. "I get the gist, but it doesn't tell me anything other than that. I have some pointers about behaviours, and that's it."

That's the problem in a sentence. Market-research personas operate at the level of symptoms. They tell you a group of people exhibit a cluster of behaviours, but they don't tell you **what produced those behaviours**, what might shift them, or what would happen if a single condition changed. For design that's not enough.

> *Designers don't just need to recognise people; we need to be able to act on the insights that tell us what is next.*

Three things are not part of market-research. I think they are becoming more and more important as the work get ore turbulent

**Origin.** Where did this behaviour come from? What was the trigger? Sometimes you'd want to design for that trigger and recreate the conditions that cause a useful habit to form. Sometimes you'd want to design around it, removing the friction that pushed someone into a workaround in the first place. Either way it's knowing the origin that gives you leverage. Without it you're staring at a snapshot.

**Trajectory.** If you know how someone arrived at this moment, you can make a reasonable guess about where they're going next. Personas should travel through time, not freeze in it. A health-nerd marathon-runner isn't a fixed type, they're someone on a path. Where does that path go? What's the next product, the next moment, the next problem they'll have? That's where the design opportunity sits.

**Contradiction.** People are messy. They say one thing and then five minutes later, under a slightly different framing, they say the opposite. Rita put it well: "It's almost like a tiny therapy session moment. They start to envelope why they would." The honest material, the stuff that actually moves design, lives in those contradictions. A persona that smooths them out has already thrown away the most useful signal you had.

## A name, and the thinking behind it

I feel like we need a new name for this view of people, Persona is an old concept. I wanted something distinct enough that it wouldn't get pulled back into the market-research orbit, but not so technical it became a different kind of jargon.

The most precise word I found is borrowed from biology: phenotype.

> *A phenotype is the observable expression of an organism, which emerges from the interaction of inheritance and environment.*

It is by definition caused, plastic, and able to express differently under different conditions. There's a whole adjacent vocabulary too (phenotypic plasticity, phenotypic drift, the extended phenotype) and the whole lot of it maps cleanly onto what designers actually need: origin, trajectory and contradiction, in three syllables.

But phenotype is a little to technical for me. It needs a sentence of setup every time you use it, and I fear a senior client will hear it as psuedo-science and jargon. But I see it as a strong underpinning rather than the name.

A name I'm more drawn to: Behavioural Arcs.

An arc has a beginning, a middle and an implied next. You can't really have one without all three, which is more or less what we want personas to do. It carries movement, it tolerates the messy bit in the middle where things bend, and a senior stakeholder can use the term without flinching. The phenotype is the engineering underneath; the arc is the word that might stick with you. And it pushes designers to think about Origin, Trajectory and Contradiction.

## The risk of replacing one rigid thing with another

The moment you name something the next generation of designers will print it on a card and defend it. People will start arguing about whether their arc is the right arc. We'll spend a few years explaining what it was supposed to mean in the first place. And before long...

> *Behavioural Arcs are dead*

That's not a reason to avoid naming it, but it is a reason to hold the name fairly lightly.

I digress.

The point isn't really the noun.

The point is that designers operate at a different level than market researchers do. We're not categorising the world, we're building things that change it, and that requires material that's generative rather than descriptive. A great designer reads a persona and asks the next question. A weaker one takes it as the answer.

## So, what?

The personas we buy from market research aren't wrong. They're just the wrong tool, built to size markets rather than to design products, and we're **still** using them as if they were the same job. For design we need material that travels through time, admits to messiness, and points at what we could build rather than only at who's already out there.

Behavioural Arcs (Aka phenotypes + a story) is a loose thought, I'd be curious what you'd call them.

## post script

In my experience, the best way to create these Behavioural Arcs is through direct Human Centred research methods: Ethnographic Interviews, expert opinions, immersive experiences. But what roles will AI tools play?

Could do the contradiction-hunting that a good interviewer does? Maybe. I'm sceptical that a tool can pick part someone's self-image without it feeling like an interrogation; a human conversation that unravels someones contradictory ideas can be very positive, but will an AI tool feel like curiosity?

That said, I can imagine a version that works better than I'd predict. People are often more honest with a chatbot than with a stranger in a room because they're not performing for anyone. The therapy frame might actually unlock more, not less.

I'm looking out for a good place to prototype that.
