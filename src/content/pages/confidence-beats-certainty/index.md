Large organisations often decide they need to become more innovative, and not in one division or one skunkworks team. They want the whole company to do it, consistently, at scale. Much of the early work goes into process. But something becomes apparent along the way that no process can fully solve.

Culture is the harder problem, and culture comes from what leaders model.

One behaviour has surfaced more than any other in this kind of work: the ability to act with confidence in the absence of certainty.

## The wrong data at the right time

These are organisations built on quantitative rigour. Commissioned research, market sizing, trend decks, statistical models. All of it is valuable. Little of it suits the early moments of an idea, when the question isn't "how big is this?" but "is there something real here?"

The shift we try to help leaders make is towards other sources of confidence: direct immersion with customers, observational research, reactions to prototypes. The data is lower fidelity. But it produces something surveys rarely do, a felt sense of belief that is specific enough to make a bold choice.

Confidence is built with the right input at the right moment, and the right inputs are often counterintuitive.

## Matching the measure to the moment

Over years of working with large organisations on innovation, one pattern repeats often enough that I've stopped being surprised by it. Teams apply late-stage rigour to early-stage ideas. A full business case is built to justify a concept that's three weeks old. A statistical confidence threshold is applied to a behaviour nobody has properly defined yet. The idea collapses under the weight of the question.

What leaders need to do instead is calibrate. The decision at each stage isn't "should we launch this?" It's "do we know enough to take the next step?" The cost of further development is not a full rollout. It is the investment needed to answer the next question.

This is the logic Steve Blank formalised in customer development, and that the Lean Startup movement carried into mainstream product thinking. Validate assumptions with the smallest experiment that generates real signal, and skip the big bets. The idea transfers directly to how large organisations manage innovation investment. Point your resources at the biggest open questions. Build enough to generate momentum and confidence, then use that momentum to unlock the next stage.

The funding follows the confidence. Leaders in large organisations can work this way, though the instinct runs the other way: to seek permission through volume of evidence rather than precision of proof. The shift is to manage confidence actively over time, in place of a single large bet on certainty that arrives too late to change anything.

## The instinct underneath the process

There is a third dimension, and I've found it the hardest to raise in a room of senior executives, because it resists the language of process and metrics entirely.

In a working session with a senior leader, one person said something that stayed with me. They had worked with many agencies, received a lot of data, and seen plenty of pictures of what was happening in the world. What they rarely got was a sense of *why* things were happening. That, they said, only came from genuine immersion: time in real contexts, understanding the texture behind an insight, staying with the material long enough for something to shift.

What they described has a name. Michael Polanyi called it tacit knowledge, the kind of understanding that can't be fully articulated and accumulates through direct experience rather than instruction. His formulation was precise: we know more than we can tell. It lives in the hands of a skilled craftsperson, or in the judgement of a clinician who can't fully explain why a diagnosis feels right, but is.

For designers this isn't a foreign idea. It's how we learn. Design education is built around tacit acquisition: you develop judgement through repeated exposure, critique and making, rather than by reading about it.

The leaders who are most effective in creative and innovation work tend to have kept this capacity alive. They still go on home visits. They talk directly to customers. They listen for stories as well as data. They have an instinct for staying calibrated, for keeping an inner sense of what's real.

Large organisations are extraordinarily good at replacing direct experience with mediated reports. Every layer of abstraction between a leader and a real person erodes a little of that calibration. Founder's instinct isn't magic. It's what is left when someone hasn't let that erosion happen.

Confidence over certainty isn't a soft preference for ambiguity. It is a discipline that lets new and disruptive ideas flourish. Like any discipline, it is built through practice and not acquired with seniority. And it doesn't come from mountains of data.
