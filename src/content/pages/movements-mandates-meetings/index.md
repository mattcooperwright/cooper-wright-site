I had a fun conversation recently with a smart, fast-growing communications company. They wanted to talk about creativity. Specifically, how to build more of it into the way their team works, and into the work itself.

It’s a question I get asked a lot. And the answer almost never starts where people expect.

## The mandate trap

Most organisations trying to change their culture reach for the same tool first: leadership communication. A vision statement. A town hall. An all-hands deck with a new set of values. The person at the top explains what they want and asks everyone to follow.

In an era of stability, that worked. When the world was predictable enough that leaders could see further than everyone else, the mandate made sense. Follow me! I know where we’re going!!

That era is gone. Thankfully.

Knowledge work doesn’t respond to mandates. You can’t tell a room of smart, curious people to be more creative and expect anything to change. You can’t instruct someone into psychological safety. The more you mandate innovation, the more you signal that it isn’t really safe to fail — because if it were, you wouldn’t need to keep saying it.

What works instead is movements. Not as a grand political metaphor, but in a very practical sense: designing the conditions that make the behaviour you want feel natural and rewarding so people choose it themselves.

The difference matters more than it might seem. A mandate says *do this*. A movement says *here’s what’s possible* and then builds the environment where people reach for it on their own terms. One produces compliance. The other produces momentum.

## Culture you can’t copy-paste

IDEO is nearly fifty years old. The reputation for creativity didn’t come from a values poster. It came from a very specific founding insight: that the best ideas emerge from diverse groups working in genuine collaboration, not from individuals handing work down a chain.

That sounds obvious now. In the 1970s, it wasn’t. The standard model for product design was sequential. Designer to engineer. Engineer to manufacturer. Lots of baton-passing, lots of translation loss. IDEO put everyone in the room together and acted in way that upheld a culture of mutual respect and curiosity that made that possible.

Actions created culture. It wasn’t decorative. It wasn’t a set of aspirational words on a wall. It was the operating condition for a method that genuinely needed it.

That’s the question I always end up asking clients: how till you act today to create the culture you want tomorrow.

For the company I was talking to, the shift they were describing (from reactive firefighting to long-term strategic partnership) demands something genuinely different from their people than what got them here.

That’s not a culture problem. That’s a capability gap dressed up as one. And the path through it isn’t a new set of values. It’s new practices, new habits, new ways of spending time together repeated often enough that the culture eventually shifts to match.

Which brings me to meetings.

## Meetings are where culture actually lives

I’ve started saying this more directly: if you want to change your culture, change your meetings.

Not the frequency. Not the length. The *design*.

Most meetings have no design at all. Some have an agenda. Almost none have a clear intended outcome, defined roles, specific interactions, or a considered arc from opening to close. We just show up and see what happens — and then wonder why the same people talk, the same ideas surface, and we leave feeling like we covered ground without actually moving.

Apply design process to meetings and the results are disproportionate to the effort involved. You don’t need to redesign everything at once. You need one or two formats that signal — this is different, someone thought about this, we’re here to *do* something, not just report on something.

Three that I come back to again and again:

**Brainstorm (but the real version).** IDEO’s brainstorm rules have been shared and borrowed so widely that most people think they know what a brainstorm is. They don’t. The rules exist because generating good ideas requires a specific environment — and that environment doesn’t happen by accident. Quantity over quality. Build on others. One conversation at a time. These aren’t nice suggestions. They’re load-bearing constraints that create the conditions for unexpected things to emerge. Run a proper brainstorm once and watch what it does to a room that thought it already knew how to have ideas.

**Pre-mortem.** Start a new project by jumping to the end and imagining it failed catastrophically. Why? What went wrong? Get every fear and doubt onto a surface — named, sorted, ranked by likelihood and impact. Then jump back to the present and ask: what can we build into the plan *now* to stop those things happening? It sounds pessimistic. It’s the opposite. It’s optimism with its eyes open. And for teams of high-achieving people who are used to performing confidence, it creates something rare: permission to say what they’re actually worried about, before it’s too late to do anything about it.

**Retrospective (focused on how, not what).** Not a post-project debrief. Not a blame session. A structured look at *how we worked* — not the content of the work. With the assumption baked in from the start that everyone did their best.

Extract the learning. Change the system. Don’t go after individuals. Done properly, retros build the kind of trust that lets you tackle tough challenges.

None of these meeting formats are complicated. All of them are commonly misunderstood. The gap between a retro that builds trust and one that quietly damages it is in the details — who runs it, how it opens, what assumptions are named at the start. Pick one up properly before you pick it up at all.

## The deeper thing underneath all of this

There’s something that connects movements, mandates and meetings.

The reason mandates don’t work on knowledge workers isn’t just about psychology. It’s about persuasion.

Aristotle had a framework for persuasion — logos, ethos, pathos.

Logic, authority, emotion. Most smart organisations are excellent at the first two. They can construct a rigorous argument. They can put credible people behind it. What they often don’t do is make you *feel* something.

And yet that’s where behaviour actually changes. Not when someone explains why something is the right thing to do. When they make you experience it first hand.

I think this is the real unlock for organisations trying to build cultures of creativity and risk. Not better logic. Not more senior sponsors. But moments — in meetings, in projects, in interactions — where people feel something different. Where the experience of working together is itself the evidence that something has changed.

There’s more to say about logos, ethos and pathos — and I’ll come back to it. But for now: if your change programme is mostly words, you’re probably only reaching the people who were already close to convinced.

## Start small. Start deliberately.

The companies I’ve seen build genuine cultures of experimentation didn’t launch transformation programmes. They ran small, well-designed experiments and then told the story of what it felt like to run them.

When leaders share the experience of trying something and failing (not the polished retrospective, but the actual discomfort) they give everyone else permission to do the same. The movement starts there. Not in the all-hands.

If you’re trying to build more creativity, more risk-taking, more emotional resonance into how your organisation works — the question isn’t *what do we mandate?*

It’s ***what*** *do we design?*
